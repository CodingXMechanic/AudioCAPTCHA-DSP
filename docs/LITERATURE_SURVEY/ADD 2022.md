# ADD 2022: The First Audio Deep Synthesis Detection Challenge

## Full reference
- Title (as printed): *ADD 2022: THE FIRST AUDIO DEEP SYNTHESIS DETECTION CHALLENGE*
- Authors (complete list as printed): Jiangyan Yi, Ruibo Fu, Jianhua Tao (corresponding author), Shuai Nie, Haoxin Ma, Chenglong Wang, Tao Wang, Zhengkun Tian, Xiaohui Zhang, Ye Bai, Cunhang Fan, Shan Liang, Shiming Wang, Shuai Zhang, Xinrui Yan, Le Xu, Zhengqi Wen, Haizhou Li, Zheng Lian, Bin Liu
- Affiliations: 1National Laboratory of Pattern Recognition, Institute of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences; 2University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China; 3Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, National University of Singapore
- Venue/year (as printed): arXiv preprint — arXiv:2202.08433v3 [cs.SD], 2 Jul 2024 (5 pages). No conference venue printed on this version. Challenge site: http://addchallenge.cn; datasets on Zenodo (records 12188127, 12188083, 10843991, 12187997, 12188035, 12188055).

## One-line contribution
Defines and reports the first Audio Deep synthesis Detection (ADD) challenge — three tracks covering low-quality fake detection (LF), partially fake detection (PF), and a generation-vs-detection rivalry game (FG) — with datasets, threshold-free EER / DSR metrics, six baselines, and results showing that no single model handles all fake scenarios and that generalization remains unsolved.

## Problem & motivation
ASVspoof challenges protect speaker-verification systems but cover limited realistic attack scenarios: (1) fake audio containing diverse background noise and music, (2) partially fake utterances where small fake clips are spliced into real speech, (3) fast-evolving TTS/VC generation algorithms. ASVspoof 2021's DF task only involves compressed audio similar to LA. ADD 2022 was launched to fill this gap with real-life, challenging fake-audio situations and to spur detection technology for deepfake/manipulated audio.

## Method (precise but simple language, 5-12 lines; key equations if present)
1. Track 1 (LF): classify bona fide vs fully fake utterances corrupted with real-world noises/background music; fakes generated by many TTS and VC systems.
2. Track 2 (PF): classify partially fake utterances (original genuine utterances manipulated with real or synthesized audio segments).
3. Track 3 (FG): two sub-tasks — FG-G generation (produce fake audio that fools the FG-D detectors, meeting text/speaker intelligibility and similarity requirements) and FG-D detection, with two evaluation rounds (R1 unseen genuine/deepfake; R2 = R1 plus FG-G submissions).
4. Metrics: threshold-free EER — P_fa(θ) = #{fake trials with score > θ}/#{total fake trials}, P_miss(θ) = #{genuine trials < θ}/#{total genuine trials}, EER at θ where P_fa = P_miss; weighted EER for FG-D: WEER = α·EER_R1 + β·EER_R2 with α = 0.4, β = 0.6; deception success rate for FG-G: DSR = W/(A·N) (W = wrong detections by all detection models at their own EER operating points, A = evaluation samples, N = detection models), with intelligibility/similarity also checked to prevent interference-sample cheating.
5. Data: training/dev from AISHELL-3 (40 male + 40 female speakers, speaker-disjoint split); fakes from mainstream TTS/VC; adaptation sets per track; test sets speaker-disjoint and without ground truth; some utterances selected from AISHELL-1/3/4.
6. Six baselines (official ASVspoof 2021 code): GMM, LCNN, RawNet2, each trained on (S01/S03/S05) training only or (S02/S04/S06) training + adaptation, with LFCC features (GMM, LCNN) or raw waveform (RawNet2); no data augmentation; tuned only on Dev.

## Key quantitative results
- Dataset statistics (Table 1): training 3,012 genuine / 24,072 fake; dev 2,307 genuine / 26,017 fake; adaptation — Track 1: 300 genuine / 700 fake; Track 2: 0 / 1,052; Track 3.2: 0 / 893.
- Test sets (Table 2): Track 1 = 109,199 utterances; Track 2 = 100,625; Track 3.2 R1 = 112,861; R2 = 126,861.
- Participation: datasets requested by more than 120 teams from 15 countries across all tracks.
- Track 1 (LF) EER (%) — Table 4: best A01 = 21.7; average of all submissions = 31.7; baselines S02 (GMM+adapt) 24.1, S01 (GMM) 25.2, S04 (LCNN+adapt) 29.9, S03 (LCNN) 32.3, S06 (RawNet2+adapt) 33.9, S05 (RawNet2) 35.2; worst A42 = 67.1. Only 3 of 42 participating teams beat the best baseline S02; GMM baselines lowest among architectures; adding adaptation data improved all Track-1 baselines.
- Track 2 (PF) EER (%) — Table 5: best B01 = 4.8; average = 37.9; baselines S01 45.8, S02 47.5, S03 47.8, S04 48.1, S05 50.1, S06 50.2; worst B27 = 57.0. 21 of 27 participating teams beat best baseline S01; GMM best again; adaptation data made all Track-2 baselines worse.
- Track 3.2 (FG-D) — Table 7: final average WEER = 34.2, lowest WEER = 10.1 (D01); R1 average EER = 20.7, lowest = 8.3 (D03); R2 average EER = 43.1, lowest = 11.0 (D02); baselines: S01 WEER 17.2, S02 16.5, S03 18.0, S04 15.0, S05 21.5, S06 20.2 (EER 100.0% = non-submission). Adding FG-G generated speech clearly degraded FG-D performance.
- Track 3.1 (FG-G) DSR (%) — Table 6: best C10 = 93.8; average = 56.1 across 14 submitting teams (e.g., C05 91.6, C14 89.5, C02/C04 72.4, worst C11 25.6).
- Conclusions printed: "difficult to use the same model to deal with all fake scenarios"; "detection generalisation remains an open problem"; evaluation metrics themselves need further discussion.

## Datasets / corpora used
Mandarin corpora AISHELL-1, AISHELL-3 (basis for training/dev: 40 male + 40 female speakers, speaker-disjoint train/dev), AISHELL-4; challenge sets: common training/dev for all tracks; per-track adaptation and test sets (unseen genuine/fake; Track 3.1 evaluation speaker IDs = 10 speakers from AISHELL-3; Track 3.2 R2 includes FG-G submissions). No speaker overlap among training/dev/adaptation/test. Publicly released on Zenodo.

## Models / systems evaluated
- Six organizer baselines: S01 GMM+LFCC (train), S02 GMM+LFCC (train+adapt), S03 LCNN+LFCC (train), S04 LCNN+LFCC (train+adapt), S05 RawNet2 raw (train), S06 RawNet2 raw (train+adapt) — code from github.com/asvspoof-challenge/2021.
- Participant submissions anonymized (A01–A42 Track 1, B01–B27 Track 2, C01–C15 Track 3.1, D01–D33 Track 3.2); architectures are not itemized beyond baseline descriptions.

## Human-study details (if any: n participants, protocol, key numbers)
None — no human listening study is reported. The only "human-side" requirement is implicit in FG-G: submitted generated samples must meet text and speaker (intelligibility/similarity) requirements evaluated by multiple methods to prevent cheating with interference samples; no participant counts or listening protocols are printed.

## Limitations acknowledged by authors
- All three tracks remain hard, especially LF (average EER 31.7% vs best 21.7%).
- Detection generalization is unsolved: the same model cannot handle all fake scenarios; detection models are easily fooled by low-quality and unseen generated fakes.
- Evaluation metrics may not be reasonable and need further discussion (generalization and metrics flagged as future focus).
- Adaptation data helps LF but hurts PF — no clear recipe for using it.
- (Implicit) test sets provided without ground truth; rankings depend on threshold-free EER/WEER conventions and the DSR definition.

## Relevance to our project (one specific paragraph)
ADD 2022 supplies the "attacker zoo" and evaluation conventions for the machine side of our framework: its LF track (fake speech buried in real-world noise and music, EER only 21.7% best / 31.7% average) is direct evidence that state-of-the-art audio classifiers degrade badly exactly in the noisy, distorted regime that our DSP distortion suite generates — the same regime in which humans remain relatively robust. For AudioCAPTCHA-DSP this matters in three ways: (1) it motivates evaluating our distorted CAPTCHAs against *both* ASR (Whisper/Vosk) and, if spoof/detection components are involved, detectors trained like the ADD baselines (GMM/LCNN/RawNet2 with LFCC), since our distortions function like "low-quality" channel conditions; (2) its metrics — threshold-free EER, WEER with α = 0.4/β = 0.6, and DSR — are the standard scores we should cite when reporting how a detector or generator behaves on our distorted stimuli, and its own caution that EER may be an unreasonable metric echoes our motivation for a human-grounded HAG metric instead of machine-only scores; (3) its partial-fake (PF) track connects to CAPTCHA-relevant localized manipulations, and the finding that no single model generalizes across fake types/noise parallels our hypothesis that no single objective metric generalizes across distortions for humans vs machines.
